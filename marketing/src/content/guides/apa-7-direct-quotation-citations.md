APA style is unusually explicit about direct quotations. It tells you when to use them, exactly where the page number goes, and how punctuation interacts with the citation. This guide covers short quotations. Long ones are in [block quote rules for APA, MLA, and Chicago](/guides/block-quote-rules-apa-mla-chicago).

Source: APA Style's pages on [quotations](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/quotations), [paraphrasing](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/paraphrasing), and [changes to quotations](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/quotations/changes), which cover Publication Manual sections 8.23–8.35.

## First: should this be a quotation?

APA says plainly that it's "best to paraphrase sources rather than directly quoting them." It lists three reasons to quote:

1. You're reproducing an **exact definition**.
2. The author said something **memorably or succinctly**.
3. You want to **respond to the exact wording**.

APA also notes that instructors and editors may limit how much quoted material you use. If a quote doesn't meet one of these three tests, paraphrase it, and see [quote or paraphrase: a decision guide](/guides/quote-or-paraphrase-decision-guide).

## The core rule

For a direct quotation, APA requires a full citation **in the same sentence** as the quotation, **including the page number** (or other location information, such as a paragraph number, when there are no pages).

*(The examples below use an invented source: Okafor, 2022, a journal article.)*

## Parenthetical citations

Put the citation immediately after the quotation, or at the end of the sentence:

> Remote onboarding programs fail most often when "managers treat the first week as orientation rather than integration" (Okafor, 2022, p. 15).

> "Managers treat the first week as orientation rather than integration" (Okafor, 2022, p. 15), which may explain the attrition pattern in our sample.

Final punctuation goes **after** the closing parenthesis, not inside the quotation marks.

## Narrative citations

Put the author and year in your sentence, and the page in parentheses after the quotation:

> Okafor (2022) found that managers "treat the first week as orientation rather than integration" (p. 15).

If the quotation comes **before** the narrative citation, put the page after the year, separated by a comma:

> "Managers treat the first week as orientation rather than integration," Okafor (2022, p. 15) concluded.

## Punctuation details

- **Periods and commas** go inside closing quotation marks. Other punctuation goes inside only when it's part of the quoted material.
- **No ellipsis** at the beginning or end of a quotation, unless the original contains one. APA's quotation page states this directly.
- **Ellipsis within** a quotation marks words you've omitted. Use four dots (a period plus an ellipsis) when the omission crosses a sentence break.
- **Square brackets** mark words you've inserted. Italics plus "[emphasis added]" mark emphasis you've added.
- **You may change without noting it:** the first letter's case, some end punctuation to fit your sentence, single vs double quotation marks, and omitted footnote callouts.

More on this in [changing quotations: ellipses, brackets, and errors](/guides/changing-quotations-ellipses-brackets).

## Page numbers, page ranges, and no pages

- One page: **p. 15**. A range: **pp. 15–16**.
- No page numbers, as in many web pages and some e-books: give other location information. APA's example is a **paragraph number**.
- Paraphrases don't require a page number, but APA says you **may** include one when it would help readers find the passage in a long or complex work.

## Worked example: fixing a draft paragraph

**Draft:**

> According to Okafor, remote onboarding fails when "managers treat the first week as orientation rather than integration". This is consistent with prior findings (Okafor, 2022).

**Problems:**
1. No year in the narrative mention ("According to Okafor").
2. No page number for the direct quotation.
3. Period outside the closing quotation mark.
4. The second citation duplicates the first and is in a different sentence from the quote.

**Fixed:**

> According to Okafor (2022), remote onboarding fails when "managers treat the first week as orientation rather than integration" (p. 15). This is consistent with earlier findings on structured mentoring (Diaz & Moreau, 2019).

(Diaz & Moreau, 2019 is also invented. Notice that the second sentence now cites the work that actually supports it.)

## The step most people skip: is it actually on page 15?

Every rule above assumes your quote and page number are right. Two common ways they go wrong:

- **You read a PDF whose viewer page count doesn't match the printed page.** "Page 3 of 22" in your viewer might be printed page 15. Cite the printed page. See [page numbers for PDFs, scans, and e-books](/guides/citing-page-numbers-pdfs-scans-ebooks).
- **You quoted from memory or from notes.** A word or two drifts ("treat" becomes "treated"). Re-check against the source before submitting.
