Every time you use a source, you choose: their words or yours. Quote too much and your paper reads like a collage. Paraphrase carelessly and you misrepresent the source or stay so close to its wording that it's a problem in its own right. Here's a test you can apply passage by passage.

## Start from APA's three reasons to quote

APA's [quotations guidance](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/quotations) prefers paraphrase in general, and gives three reasons to quote instead:

1. You're reproducing an **exact definition**.
2. The author said something **memorably or succinctly**.
3. You want to **respond to the exact wording**.

These reasons hold up well beyond APA. In humanities fields that analyze texts closely, reason 3 comes up constantly, because the wording is the evidence. In empirical fields, findings usually matter more than phrasing, so paraphrase dominates. APA's [paraphrasing page](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/paraphrasing) notes that published authors paraphrase most of the time and encourages students to do the same.

## The four-question test

Ask these in order:

1. **Will I analyze the specific words?** → Quote.
2. **Is it a definition, a term of art, or a contested phrase?** → Quote, at least the key phrase.
3. **Would my paraphrase be clearly shorter, clearer, or better fitted to my point?** → Paraphrase.
4. **Can I paraphrase it without the source open in front of me, and still get it right?** If not, you don't understand it well enough yet. Re-read before you do either.

If you reach the end with no clear answer, paraphrase and cite. You can always add a short quoted phrase for flavor.

## Worked example: one passage, four treatments

*(Invented original, Okafor 2022, p. 15.)*

> **Original:** "In every firm we studied, managers described the first week as a checklist of accounts, trainings, and introductions; few described it as the start of a working relationship."

**A. Too close (a problem even with a citation).**

> In all the firms studied, managers saw the first week as a list of accounts, trainings, and introductions, and few saw it as the beginning of a working relationship (Okafor, 2022).

This keeps the original's structure and most of its words, with a few swapped synonyms. Many instructors treat this as improper source use whether or not it's cited. It's neither a quotation nor your own phrasing.

**B. Distorted.**

> Okafor (2022) found that managers don't care about building relationships with new hires.

This is your words, but not the source's claim. The source describes how managers *described* the first week, not what they care about.

**C. Good paraphrase.**

> Okafor (2022) found that managers tended to treat onboarding as an administrative task rather than the beginning of a relationship with the new employee.

This uses a new structure and new wording, and keeps the same claim at the same strength.

**D. Paraphrase plus a quoted phrase.**

> Okafor (2022) found that managers treated new hires' first week as administrative, "a checklist of accounts, trainings, and introductions" (p. 15), rather than the start of a working relationship.

This quotes only the vivid phrase, which earns its place under reason 2, and paraphrases the rest.

C or D is right here. A and B are the two failure modes to watch for.

## How to paraphrase without drifting

1. **Read the passage, then look away.**
2. **Say the point out loud or write it from memory**, in your own structure.
3. **Compare with the original** for accuracy: same claim, same strength, same scope. Did "few" become "no"? Did "in this sample" disappear?
4. **Compare for closeness:** if more than a short run of distinctive words matches, rewrite or quote those words.
5. **Cite.** A paraphrase still needs a citation.

## Citation rules for paraphrases (APA)

From APA's [paraphrasing page](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/paraphrasing):

- **A page number isn't required**, but you may include one when it helps readers find the passage in a long or complex work.
- **For a long paraphrase,** cite on first mention. You don't need to repeat the citation in following sentences as long as it's clear the same work continues.
- **For a new paragraph,** reintroduce the citation.
- **When switching among sources,** repeat citations so each claim's source is clear.
- **If you read the idea in a secondary source,** read and cite the original if possible. Otherwise use a secondary citation (see [citing a source quoted in another source](/guides/citing-a-source-quoted-in-another-source)).

MLA and Chicago also require citations for paraphrase. Including a page reference is good practice in both, because it lets readers check your reading.

## A quick audit of your draft

Highlight every direct quotation in one section of your draft. For each one, write which of APA's three reasons it serves. Any quote with no reason is a candidate for paraphrase.

Then pick three paraphrases and run step 3 above against the original. Paraphrase drift is invisible from inside your own draft. You only see it next to the source.
