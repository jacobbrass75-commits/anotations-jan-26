A **reverse outline** is an outline written after the draft. You go paragraph by paragraph and write down what each one actually says. Purdue OWL describes [reverse outlining](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/the_writing_process/reverse_outlining.html) as a way to take notes and revise your own work, starting with the topic of each paragraph. Add one more column, the evidence behind each claim, and it becomes a strong tool for finding weak spots in a long argument.

## The method

Make a table with four columns, one row per paragraph:

| ¶ | Claim (one sentence, your words) | Evidence cited | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|

1. **Claim.** What does this paragraph assert? One sentence. If you can't write it, the paragraph doesn't have a clear claim yet. That's a finding.
2. **Evidence cited.** List the sources and passages cited for that claim, with pages.
3. **Strength.** Rate each row:
   - **S (supported):** the cited passages directly establish the claim.
   - **P (partial):** the passages support part of it, or a narrower version.
   - **U (unsupported):** no citation, or the citation doesn't establish the claim.
   - **C (context):** background that doesn't need strong support.

Rate honestly. Open the source when you're not sure.

## Worked example: one section, eight paragraphs

*(Invented section: "Unions and wage floors before 1938.")*

| ¶ | Claim | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Federal minimum wage law came in 1938 | General knowledge; textbook | C |
| 2 | Dockworkers' groups set wage floors decades earlier | Ortiz p. 142 | P: Ortiz limits this to single-employer ports |
| 3 | These floors were enforced through strikes | Ortiz p. 157; Price p. 77 | S |
| 4 | Employers accepted them because of labor shortages | — | **U** |
| 5 | Agreements spread from Galveston to other ports | Ortiz p. 160 | S |
| 6 | Public opinion favored dockworkers | Lin p. 41 (secondary, pamphlet) | P: secondary; one source |
| 7 | This pattern contradicts the standard narrative | Ortiz p. 9 | P: Ortiz's argument, not independent evidence |
| 8 | Therefore, labor law followed practice rather than leading it | ¶2–7 | Depends on ¶2, ¶4, ¶6 |

## Reading the audit

**Unsupported claims (U).** Paragraph 4 makes a causal claim with nothing behind it. Your options:
- find evidence (a labor-market source, port records),
- soften the claim ("may have accepted them, possibly because..."), or
- cut it.

**Partial support (P).** These are the most valuable rows, because they're where you over-claim without noticing.
- ¶2 needs the scope qualifier the source gives (see the worked example in [quote tracking from highlight to final draft](/guides/quote-tracking-from-highlight-to-final-draft)).
- ¶6 relies on one secondary citation. Try to find the original pamphlet, or add a second source.
- ¶7 cites an author's *argument* as if it were *evidence*. Either present it as Ortiz's interpretation ("Ortiz argues...") or add independent evidence.

**Conclusions resting on weak rows.** ¶8 depends on ¶2, ¶4, and ¶6, all rated P or U. Your section conclusion is only as strong as its weakest premise. Fix those first.

**Source concentration.** Count the citations. Here, Ortiz appears in 5 of 8 paragraphs. Leaning that heavily on one source means your section is partly a summary of Ortiz. Look for corroborating or competing sources, which a [literature review evidence matrix](/guides/literature-review-evidence-matrix) can help you find.

## Decision rules

- **Every U in a topic sentence or conclusion** gets fixed before submission. No exceptions.
- **A P that supports a conclusion** should become an S or be reworded to match its support.
- **Any source cited in more than half the paragraphs** of a section needs a second voice nearby.
- **An Argument cited as Evidence** (an author's interpretation used as fact) gets reframed with "argues" or "contends."

## When to run it

- **After a full draft of a section**, before line editing. There's no point polishing a paragraph you'll cut.
- **Again before submission**, for any section you substantially revised.
- **When an advisor says "this needs more support"** but doesn't say where. The audit shows you where.

## Doing it with ScholarMark

The audit is a thinking exercise, and it works with a spreadsheet and your sources. ScholarMark helps in two places:

- **Filling the Evidence column.** If your highlights are labeled with notes that name the claim they support (for example, "supports §2 wage floor claim"), project search for the claim words brings up the candidate passages across all sources. Seeing the Methodology and Argument labels next to Evidence makes the "argument cited as evidence" problem easy to spot.
- **A second reader for U and P rows.** Verify Paper, an AI review of a compiled draft against your project sources, is designed to flag unsupported or over-claimed assertions. Compare its findings with your own ratings. Where you disagree, re-open the source.
