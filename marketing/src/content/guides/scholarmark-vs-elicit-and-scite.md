**Disclosure:** ScholarMark publishes this guide. Elicit and scite are described here using claims from their own homepages, [elicit.com](https://elicit.com/) and [scite.ai](https://scite.ai/). Their numbers are their claims, not ScholarMark's measurements.

These three tools get compared because all three involve AI and research papers. They sit at different stages:

1. **Discovery:** what has been published on my question? (Elicit, scite)
2. **Evaluation:** how has this paper been received? (scite)
3. **Use:** exactly what does my source say, and does my draft represent it correctly? (ScholarMark)

## What Elicit says it does

- **Search:** "over 138 million academic papers and 545,000 clinical trials," using semantic search so you don't need exact keywords.
- **Research reports:** structured briefs "inspired by systematic reviews," customizable by papers and covered information.
- **Systematic reviews:** automated screening and data extraction, with partial support for search and report generation.
- **Research agent:** gathers evidence across databases, journals, public data, and uploaded documents.

Elicit's strength is breadth and structure across many papers at once, especially in empirical fields.

## What scite says it does

- **Smart Citations:** show how later research has "supported, challenged, or discussed" a paper or claim.
- **Full-text search** across what it describes as 300M+ articles, preprints, books, patents, and datasets.
- **An AI assistant** that links claims back to sentences in specific papers.
- **Integrations:** a browser extension, a Zotero plugin, an API, and an MCP server for Claude, ChatGPT, and other assistants.

scite's distinctive job is evaluation: has this finding held up?

## What ScholarMark does

ScholarMark doesn't search the literature. It starts after you've chosen sources:

- Upload PDFs, text, or image scans into a project.
- Highlight exact passages as Key Quote, Evidence, Argument, Methodology, or Your Note, each with your reasoning.
- Search across your own sources and notes.
- Generate Chicago notes-bibliography, MLA, or APA citations.
- Draft with quotes that link back to the saved passage, export DOCX, and run an AI Verify Paper review that flags quotes, paraphrases, and citations that don't match your sources.

## Which job do you have?

| If you're asking... | Use |
|---|---|
| "What research exists on X?" | Elicit, scite, Google Scholar, library databases |
| "Is this 2015 finding still accepted?" | scite (Smart Citations), plus a review article |
| "Extract sample sizes from 40 RCTs" | Elicit |
| "Where exactly did this author say that, on which page?" | ScholarMark (or your PDF reader) |
| "Does my chapter quote and paraphrase accurately?" | ScholarMark's Verify Paper, plus your own check |
| "Format my footnotes in Chicago" | ScholarMark or a reference manager |

## How they connect

A typical pipeline:

1. **Search** in Elicit or scite to build a candidate list.
2. **Screen** by reading abstracts, and check with scite how key findings have been cited.
3. **Get the full text** through your library. Discovery tools often surface papers you'll still need institutional access to read.
4. **Store** the papers you keep: records in a reference manager, plus full text for the ones you'll quote in ScholarMark.
5. **Read and highlight** in ScholarMark, filing passages with notes tied to your argument.
6. **Draft and verify** against those saved passages.

AI summaries from discovery tools are a starting point, not something to cite. If a report says "Study A found X," find X in Study A itself before you write it. APA's guidance makes the same point about secondary sources: [find, read, and cite the primary source](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/secondary-sources) whenever possible.

## Worked example: from a discovery claim to a citable sentence

Say you're writing a literature review on spaced repetition in medical education.

**In Elicit:** a report summarizes 15 studies and says one trial found improved retention at six months.

**The trap:** writing "spaced repetition improved six-month retention (Author, Year)" from the summary alone. The summary may have compressed a subgroup result, a secondary outcome, or a non-significant trend.

**In scite:** you check how later papers cite that trial. Several mention it, one contrasts it, and none directly replicates it. That shapes how strongly you phrase the claim.

**In ScholarMark:** you upload the trial's PDF, find the results paragraph, and highlight the sentence reporting the six-month outcome as Evidence. Your note reads: "Secondary outcome; n = 64; don't present as primary finding. Printed p. 7."

**Your sentence:** *In one trial, a secondary outcome showed higher retention at six months for the spaced-repetition group (Author, Year), although this has not been directly replicated.*

That sentence is supported by the passage itself, not by a summary of it.

## Common mistakes

- **Citing the AI report instead of the paper.** Your citation should point to what you read and verified.
- **Treating "supported by later research" as proof.** A supporting citation tells you another paper agreed. Read it before leaning on that.
- **Skipping full text.** Abstracts often leave out the caveats that matter for accurate paraphrase. See [quote or paraphrase: a decision guide](/guides/quote-or-paraphrase-decision-guide).
