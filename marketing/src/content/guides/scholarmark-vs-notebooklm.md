**Disclosure:** ScholarMark publishes this guide. NotebookLM details below come from Google's own help center, linked inline. Google's help pages now call the product **Gemini Notebook**, and notebooklm.google.com redirects to notebook.google.com. This guide uses both names because most people still search for NotebookLM.

Both tools take your sources and let AI work from them, so they're easy to confuse. They're built for different moments in a research project.

- **NotebookLM / Gemini Notebook** helps you *understand* a set of sources: ask questions, get summaries, generate study guides, briefings, audio overviews, and mind maps.
- **ScholarMark** helps you *use* sources in a long piece of writing: keep exact quotes with context, organize evidence, format Chicago, MLA, or APA citations, draft, and review the draft against the sources.

## What Google documents about NotebookLM

From Google's [source documentation](https://support.google.com/notebooklm/answer/16215270?hl=en) and [FAQ](https://support.google.com/notebooklm/answer/16269187?hl=en):

- **Source types:** PDFs, Word (.docx), text, Markdown, CSV, PowerPoint, Google Docs/Slides/Sheets, images, audio files, web URLs, public YouTube URLs, EPUB, pasted text, and eligible Play Books.
- **Limits:** up to 500,000 words or 200MB per uploaded source, and up to 50 sources per notebook for free users.
- **Grounding:** answers draw on your sources, with "clear in-line citations." The FAQ notes that if a source is very short, it may reference the whole document rather than a specific passage.
- **Drive sync:** Google Drive sources re-sync automatically.
- **Copy protection:** copy-protected PDFs can't be imported.

That's a capable reading tool. Many students use it well for exam prep and for getting oriented in a new literature.

## What ScholarMark does

- **Projects** for a paper, thesis, or chapter, with a working thesis and scope you can record.
- **Sources:** PDF, TXT, and images (including HEIC phone photos), with OCR for scans. Several page photos can be combined into one source. There's no .docx upload, so paste the text or export to PDF first.
- **Categorized highlights:** Key Quote, Evidence, Argument, Methodology, or Your Note, each with your note. The AI can propose highlights against a research goal you state.
- **Citations:** saved metadata with Chicago notes-bibliography (full and shortened notes plus bibliography), MLA, and APA output.
- **Drafting:** quotes in a draft that match saved highlights link back to the source passage. The draft exports to DOCX.
- **Verify Paper:** an AI review of a compiled draft against project sources. It flags quotes that don't match, paraphrases that drift, citations with no matching source, and formatting inconsistencies. It's a second reader, not a guarantee.

## The key difference: in-line references vs citations you can submit

A NotebookLM answer can point you to the passage it drew from. That's valuable for understanding. Your paper, though, needs a citation in a specific style, an exact quotation, and the printed page number from the edition you used. In the Google help pages cited here, the citations described are in-line references back to your sources, not formatted bibliography entries.

ScholarMark starts from the other end. The unit of work is a saved, exact passage with source metadata and your note, which becomes a citation in your style.

## Decision criteria

**NotebookLM is the better fit when:**
- You're early in a project and need to understand material fast.
- Your sources include formats ScholarMark doesn't accept, such as audio, YouTube, or Slides.
- You want study outputs: guides, briefings, audio overviews.
- You don't need formatted citations from the tool.

**ScholarMark is the better fit when:**
- You're drafting something long that must quote accurately and cite in Chicago, MLA, or APA.
- Your evidence comes from scans or archive photos that need OCR.
- You want a persistent quote bank that outlives any single chat.
- You want a review pass that checks your draft against the sources you actually uploaded.

**Use both when:** you explore in NotebookLM, then move the sources you'll quote into ScholarMark and build the evidence trail there.

## Worked example: same sources, different jobs

You have 12 sources on community land trusts for a 25-page policy paper.

**Week 1, in NotebookLM.** You ask, "What are the main disagreements across these sources about resale formulas?" The answer summarizes three positions and cites the passages it used. You now know which four sources matter most.

**Week 2, in ScholarMark.** You upload those four sources to a project. In one report, you highlight the sentence that defines the "shared appreciation" formula and file it as Key Quote, with the note "Definition for section 2; printed p. 14." You file a passage reporting resale data as Evidence: "Use for claim about affordability retention; check the year of the data."

**Week 4, drafting.** You write: *As the report defines it, the formula "..."* The quotation links back to the highlight, so you can confirm the wording in one click.

**Before submitting.** You run Verify Paper on the section. Say it flags a paraphrase that states a retention rate the source gives only for one city. You open the source and narrow your sentence.

NotebookLM helped you know which sources to read. ScholarMark helped you prove what you claimed.

## What to watch for with any AI source tool

- **"Grounded" isn't "verified."** Both tools work from your sources, but the model can still misread, over-generalize, or attach a claim to the wrong passage. Check quotations against the source yourself.
- **Mind the edition.** If you uploaded a preprint, your page numbers and wording may not match the published version you cite. See [tracking editions, translations, and versions](/guides/tracking-editions-translations-versions).
- **Check your course policy** on AI-assisted reading and drafting before you rely on either tool.
