The most common literature review problem isn't too few sources. It's paragraphs that summarize one source at a time: "Smith found... Jones found... Lee found..." Reviewers call this a list, not a review. An evidence matrix fixes it by forcing you to compare sources on the same dimensions before you write.

## What an evidence matrix is

A table where:
- each **row** is a source,
- each **column** is a question you ask of every source,
- each **cell** holds a short answer, with a page reference for anything you might quote.

## Columns that work

Start with these and adjust for your field:

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **Source** | Author, year, short title |
| **Question** | What the source set out to answer, in one line |
| **Method / evidence base** | Sample, data, archive, corpus, or theoretical approach |
| **Main finding or claim** | One or two lines, in your words |
| **Key passage** | The exact sentence you'd quote, with its printed page |
| **Limitations** | As stated by the author, plus any you notice |
| **Relation to my question** | Supports, complicates, contradicts, or provides context |
| **Theme tags** | Two or three labels you'll group by later |

The **Relation** and **Theme** columns turn summaries into a review. Fill them in every time.

## Worked example: four sources

*Research question (invented): Does structured peer mentoring improve first-year retention at commuter colleges?* All four sources are invented for illustration.

| Source | Method | Finding | Key passage | Limitations | Relation | Themes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hamid et al. 2018 | Quasi-experiment, 1 campus, n = 410 | Course completion improved; retention unchanged | "no detectable difference in second-fall enrollment" (p. 212) | Single campus; residential | Complicates | outcome-choice, residential |
| Nwosu et al. 2020 | RCT, 3 commuter campuses, n = 1,150 | Retention up for students attending 4+ sessions | "11 percentage points higher among students who attended four or more sessions" (p. 9) | Self-selected attendance | Supports (conditional) | dosage, commuter |
| Ortega 2021 | Interviews, 38 students | Mentoring valued for logistics, not belonging | "they wanted someone who knew which office to call" (p. 54) | Small, qualitative | Context | mechanism, commuter |
| Ruiz & Park 2023 | Meta-analysis, 22 studies | Small average effect; larger with structured contact | "effects were concentrated in programs with scheduled weekly contact" (p. 18) | Few commuter-specific studies | Supports (conditional) | dosage, structure |

## From matrix to paragraphs

Read **down the theme columns**, not across the rows.

**Theme: dosage.** Nwosu and Ruiz & Park both tie the effect to how much contact students get. That's a paragraph.

> Evidence for a retention effect is strongest where mentoring involves sustained, scheduled contact. In a three-campus trial, retention was "11 percentage points higher among students who attended four or more sessions" (Nwosu et al., 2020, p. 9), and a meta-analysis of 22 studies found that "effects were concentrated in programs with scheduled weekly contact" (Ruiz & Park, 2023, p. 18). Because attendance in the trial was self-selected, however, the dosage pattern may partly reflect which students chose to attend.

**Theme: outcome choice.** Hamid shows that what you measure matters: completion improved, retention didn't. That's a second paragraph, and a reason to be precise about outcomes in your own claims.

**Theme: commuter context.** Ortega's interviews suggest the *mechanism* at commuter colleges may be practical navigation rather than belonging. That's a third paragraph, and possibly your contribution.

Notice that no paragraph is about a single source, and every sentence that makes a claim points to a passage you can find.

## How many columns and rows?

- **Columns:** 6–9. More than that and you stop filling them in.
- **Rows:** everything you'll cite for substance. Background sources you cite once in passing can stay out.
- **Cells:** keep them short. If a cell needs a paragraph, that's a note, not a matrix entry.

## Where to keep the matrix

**A spreadsheet** works for many projects. Its weakness is that the "key passage" column holds text copied away from its source, so it can drift if you re-type it or the edition changes.

**A systematic-review tool** fits if you're extracting the same data points from dozens of empirical studies. [Elicit](https://elicit.com/), for example, describes automated screening and data extraction for systematic reviews.

**ScholarMark** can hold the evidence side of the matrix: each source in a project, each key passage highlighted and filed as Evidence, Argument, or Methodology, with your relation and theme tags written in the note (for example, "Supports-conditional; dosage; commuter; p. 9"). Project search for "dosage" then pulls every passage tagged with it across all sources. The passages stay attached to their sources, which keeps the key-passage column honest. You may still want a spreadsheet for the at-a-glance grid.

## Common mistakes

- **Filling the Finding column with the abstract.** Abstracts overstate. Read the results.
- **Leaving Relation blank** because "it's just background." Context is a relation too.
- **Quoting without pages** in the Key passage column. You'll need them later.
- **Writing from rows.** If your paragraph's topic sentence names an author, you're summarizing. Name a theme instead.
