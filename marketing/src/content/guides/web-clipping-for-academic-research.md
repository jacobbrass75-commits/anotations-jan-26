A bookmark saves an address. Research needs the evidence: the exact passage you'll rely on, what surrounded it, who published it, and when. Web pages change and disappear, and "it said that when I read it" won't persuade a reader.

## What to capture for every web source

| Field | Why |
|---|---|
| **Selected passage, exact** | The evidence itself |
| **Surrounding context** | So you can tell later whether it was the author's claim, a quotation, or a counterpoint |
| **URL** | Where it lives (or lived) |
| **Page title** | For the citation, from the page heading, not the browser tab |
| **Site / publisher** | For the citation's container or publisher element |
| **Author** | If listed, including organizational authors |
| **Publication or "last updated" date** | Styles use these differently (see below) |
| **Date you captured it** | Your access date, and your evidence of when the page said this |
| **Your note** | Why it matters and which claim it supports |

**Why both dates matter:** Chicago (per [Purdue OWL's CMOS 18 page](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/chicago_manual_18th_edition/cmos_formatting_and_style_guide/web_sources.html)) uses an access date only when no other date is available. MLA treats it as [optional but useful](https://style.mla.org/access-dates/), especially for undated or changeable pages. Capture both, then let your style decide which appears. For missing authors and dates, see [citing web sources with missing author or date](/guides/citing-web-sources-missing-author-date).

## Pages change: protect the evidence

- **Clip the passage text**, not just the link. If the page is edited, you still have what you read.
- **Note "last updated" stamps** when you see them.
- **For pages central to your argument,** consider saving an archived snapshot too, so a reader can see the version you cited.
- **News articles get corrected.** If a correction notice appears later, re-check your quote against the current version and decide which version you're citing.

## Choosing a clipping tool

Different tools capture different things:

- **[Zotero Connector](https://www.zotero.org/support/adding_items_to_zotero)** saves bibliographic items (and often snapshots or PDFs) from sites it recognizes. It's built for references.
- **[Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is/)** adds highlights and comments over web pages, with an emphasis on social, course-based annotation.
- **[Readwise Reader](https://readwise.io/read)** is a read-it-later app with web highlighting through its browser extension, alongside PDFs, EPUBs, and feeds.
- **ScholarMark's Chrome extension** saves selected passages as research clips, with citation metadata, into your ScholarMark account.

Choose by what you'll do next. If the next step is a bibliography, use a reference manager's connector. If it's discussion, use social annotation. If it's quoting the passage in a draft, use a passage-level clipper.

## How ScholarMark's clipper works

From the extension's documentation:

- Select text on a page, then choose **Save to ScholarMark** from the right-click menu, or use the keyboard shortcut.
- The extension saves the **selected passage, page URL, page title, site name, author and publication-date metadata when the page provides it, surrounding context, a category, and your default project**.
- It reads the page **only when you trigger a save**. It doesn't collect browsing content in the background.
- Clips appear in **Web Clips** in the app, where you can search, review, cite, and **promote** them into a research project.

It's a Chrome extension, so other browsers need a different capture method.

## Worked example: clipping for a policy paper

You're writing about municipal heat-emergency plans.

**Source 1:** a city government page with no author and a "Last updated" stamp.
- You select the paragraph defining the city's heat-alert threshold and save it.
- The clip stores the passage, URL, title, and site name. No author metadata exists, and the publication date is captured if the page exposes it.
- In the clip's note, you add: "Last updated Aug 2026 per footer. Threshold definition for §1." You'll cite the city as the author per your style, and use the update date.

**Source 2:** a news article about a heat-related hospital surge.
- You select the sentence with the hospital admission figure and save it. Author and publication date come through from the page's metadata.
- Your note: "Figure attributed to county health dept. Find the original report before citing the number."
- That note prompts a second search. You find the county's report and cite the number from it directly, keeping the news article for context.

**Source 3:** a think-tank report as a PDF.
- Don't clip it. Download the PDF and upload it to your project as a source, so you keep page numbers for citation.

## After clipping: triage weekly

Clips pile up. Once a week:

1. **Promote** clips you'll use into the project for the paper they belong to.
2. **Add or fix notes** while you still remember why you saved them.
3. **Chase originals** for any clip that reports someone else's data or quotes (see [citing a source quoted in another source](/guides/citing-a-source-quoted-in-another-source)).
4. **Delete** what you won't use.
