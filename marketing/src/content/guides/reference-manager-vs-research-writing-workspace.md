"Which tool should I use for my thesis?" is really two questions:

1. Where do the **records** of what I've read live, and how do they become citations?
2. Where does the **evidence** live, meaning the specific passages my argument depends on, and how does it get into my draft accurately?

Tools built for the first question are **reference managers**. Tools built for the second are what this guide calls **research writing workspaces**. Some overlap, but each is organized around a different unit.

## The unit of work

| | Reference manager | Writing workspace |
|---|---|---|
| Basic unit | A bibliographic record (one work) | A passage (one quote or finding) |
| Organized by | Collections, tags, folders | Projects, claims, sections |
| Main output | Formatted citations and bibliography | A draft grounded in specific passages |
| Typical integration | Word / Google Docs / LaTeX plugins | A drafting surface plus export |
| Failure it prevents | Inconsistent or incomplete citations | Misquotes, lost pages, drifting paraphrase |

Examples of reference managers include [Zotero](https://www.zotero.org/), [EndNote](https://endnote.com/), [Mendeley](https://www.elsevier.com/products/mendeley), and [Paperpile](https://paperpile.com/). ScholarMark is a writing workspace. Reference managers increasingly offer annotation, and Zotero's PDF annotations can be [pulled into notes with links back to the PDF page](https://www.zotero.org/support/pdf_reader), so the line isn't absolute. The organizing unit is still different.

## Why the distinction matters in long projects

In a 10-page paper with eight sources, you can hold the evidence in your head. In an 80-page thesis with 60 sources, several things change:

- **Recall decays.** You'll highlight something in October and draft it in February.
- **Versions multiply.** Preprint vs published article, first vs second edition, original vs translation.
- **Claims get revised.** Your chapter 2 argument shifts, and some evidence stops fitting.
- **Paraphrase drifts.** Each rewrite moves your sentence further from what the source actually said.

A reference manager keeps the bibliography right through all of this. It doesn't, by design, tell you that your paraphrase on page 31 now overstates the source. That's an evidence problem.

## Self-assessment: score your project

Give yourself one point for each statement that's true.

**Reference-manager signals**
- [ ] I have more than 40 works to cite.
- [ ] My required style isn't Chicago, MLA, or APA, or it's a journal-specific variant.
- [ ] I write in Word, Google Docs, or LaTeX and want citations to update automatically.
- [ ] I share a bibliography with co-authors or a lab.
- [ ] I'll reuse this library across future papers.

**Writing-workspace signals**
- [ ] More than a third of my paragraphs will include a direct quotation.
- [ ] Many sources are scans, archive photos, or long books.
- [ ] I've lost a quote or a page number before and had to re-find it.
- [ ] My advisor has flagged paraphrases as inaccurate or too close.
- [ ] I'll draft over several months and need to remember why each passage mattered.

**Reading your score**
- **Mostly reference-manager points:** start with a reference manager. A workspace is optional.
- **Mostly workspace points:** start with a workspace. Add a reference manager if you need plugin-managed citations or a style the workspace doesn't support.
- **High on both:** use both, and give each a clear job (records vs evidence). Pick one tool as the source of truth for bibliographic metadata.

## Worked example: two students, same department

**Student A**, comparative literature, Chicago notes-bibliography. Score: 1 reference-manager point (works to cite) and 5 workspace points.
- *Setup:* ScholarMark as the primary tool, with sources uploaded per chapter, quotes filed with page notes, and Chicago notes and bibliography generated there. Drafts export to DOCX.
- *Why not a reference manager first?* Formatting citations isn't their risk. Misquoting a translated novel is.

**Student B**, education policy, APA 7, co-authoring a chapter. Score: 4 reference-manager points and 1 workspace point.
- *Setup:* Zotero with a shared group library and the Word plugin. No workspace.
- *Why?* They paraphrase much more than they quote, as [APA's guidance](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/paraphrasing) encourages. Collaboration and formatting are their risks.

Same department, opposite setups, and both are right.

## What a writing workspace should do

If you score high on workspace signals, judge any tool, mine included, on these:

1. **Exact text is preserved.** Highlights store the source's wording, not a summary of it.
2. **Context travels with the quote.** You can get from a quote back to its surrounding paragraph in one step.
3. **Notes capture purpose.** Each saved passage records why you saved it and what claim it supports.
4. **The draft links to the evidence.** You can trace a sentence back to its passage.
5. **There's a review step.** Something checks quotes and citations against the sources before submission, and you check what it flags.
6. **Your locators are yours.** The page number you cite should come from the printed page you consulted. See [page numbers for PDFs, scans, and e-books](/guides/citing-page-numbers-pdfs-scans-ebooks).

## What a reference manager should do

1. **Clean capture** from databases and catalogs, with editable metadata.
2. **Style coverage** for the style you need, in the edition you need.
3. **Live citing** in your word processor.
4. **Duplicate handling** so the same work isn't cited two ways.
5. **Export and portability** so you're not locked in.
