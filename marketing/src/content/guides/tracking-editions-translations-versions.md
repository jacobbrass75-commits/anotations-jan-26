"I quoted it exactly" can still be wrong if you quoted a different version than the one you cite. Wording changes between a preprint and its published version, between editions, and between translations. Pagination changes even more often. For quotation accuracy, the version is part of the source.

## Where versions diverge

| Situation | What usually differs |
|---|---|
| **Preprint vs published article** | Wording (after copyediting and review), pagination, sometimes findings |
| **First vs revised edition** | Added or cut chapters, revised arguments, new pagination |
| **Hardcover vs paperback / reprint** | Often only pagination and date, but sometimes corrections |
| **Original vs translation** | Every word of a quotation, plus pagination, plus translator's choices |
| **Print vs digitized edition** | Pagination may be preserved or lost, and the record's date may be the digitization date |
| **Web page, over time** | Anything; edits are often unannounced |

One real case of how records can differ: [how to verify AI-generated citations](/guides/how-to-verify-ai-generated-citations) describes a Crossref record whose date differs from the publication year a style guide's example uses for the same book. Records describe particular printings and editions.

## The rule: cite what you read

Your citation tells the reader where to find the words you quoted. So:

- **Cite the version you actually read and quoted from.**
- If you read a preprint and later get the published version, **re-check your quotations and page numbers** against the published one before switching the citation.
- If you read a translation, **cite the translation** (with translator) and quote its wording.

On DOIs, APA says to [include a DOI for any work that has one](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/references/dois-urls), whether you used the online or print version. Make sure the DOI belongs to the version you're citing. Preprints and published articles usually have different identifiers.

## What to record for every source

At intake, capture:

- **Edition statement** ("2nd ed.," "revised edition," "Anniversary edition"), from the title page or copyright page, not a bookstore listing.
- **Year of the version you used**, and the original publication year if your style or argument needs it.
- **Translator**, for translated works.
- **Version identifier** for articles: preprint server and version number, or the journal DOI.
- **Date accessed** and any "last updated" date for web pages.

MLA's core elements include **Version** as its own element ([Purdue OWL MLA overview](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/mla_style/mla_formatting_and_style_guide/mla_formatting_and_style_guide.html)), which is where edition statements go. Chicago and APA include edition information in the reference too.

## Worked example 1: a preprint you read in March

In March, you read a preprint, quote one sentence, and note page 12. In May, the article is published.

1. Open the published version and find your quoted sentence. Say it now reads "may contribute to" where the preprint said "drives." That's a substantive change.
2. Decide which to cite. Most instructors and journals expect the published version when one exists. Quote its wording ("may contribute to"), and adjust your sentence. The stronger claim is no longer supported.
3. Find the new page number. Published pagination usually differs from a preprint's.
4. Update the citation and DOI to the published version.
5. Keep a note on the source: "Switched from preprint v2 to published, May 2026; quote rechecked."

## Worked example 2: a translated novel

You're writing in English about a novel originally in Spanish, and reading a published English translation.

- **Quote the translation's wording**, and cite the translation, including the translator.
- **If you compare with the original,** quote the original in its language alongside, following your style's rules. MLA's answer on [quotations and translations](https://style.mla.org/quotations-and-translations/) (written for the eighth edition) covers when to set them as block quotations.
- **If you translate a passage yourself,** say so ("my translation"), and don't cite a published translation for words that aren't in it.
- **Don't mix translations.** Quoting two different translators' versions under one citation is a common, hard-to-spot error.

## Worked example 3: two editions on your shelf

You own the first edition of a monograph. The library copy you scanned is the revised second edition.

- Pick **one** edition for all quotations from this work, ideally the one your field treats as standard, or the most recent.
- If you must quote both (for instance, because your argument is about how the author revised the book), make them **two separate records**, clearly labeled by edition, and cite each where you use it.

- Note version switches in the source's notes, as in the preprint example above.

## Checklist

- [ ] Every source records the edition or version used.
- [ ] Translated works record the translator, and quotes match that translation.
- [ ] No quotation comes from a different version than its citation.
- [ ] Each DOI belongs to the cited version.
- [ ] Any version switch has been followed by re-checking quotes and pages.
