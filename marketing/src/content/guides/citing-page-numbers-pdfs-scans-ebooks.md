A large share of citation errors aren't formatting mistakes. They're wrong locators: the page number in the citation doesn't match the page in the source. Most come from one confusion: **the page number your software shows isn't the page number printed on the source.**

## The rule

Cite the **printed page number** of the version you used, meaning the number on the page itself, in the header or footer. Not the viewer's page count, not the file's page index, not "page 3 of 22."

Why: your reader will look up the published version, not your file. The printed number is the one they can find.

## Case 1: a journal article PDF

The article runs pages 201–222 in the journal. Your PDF has 22 pages, plus a cover sheet the database added.

- The viewer shows "6 / 23" (cover sheet plus five pages in).
- The printed header on that page says **205**.
- **Cite 205.**

**Quick check:** look at the first page of the article itself. If it's printed 201, then printed page = viewer page − 1 (for the cover sheet) + 200. Work it out once per file and write it in your notes.

## Case 2: a scanned book chapter with two-page spreads

Someone scanned a chapter flat, so each image shows two book pages side by side.

- Viewer page 12 shows printed pages 88 (left) and 89 (right).
- Your quote is in the right-hand column, so **cite 89**.
- If the quote runs across the gutter from 88 to 89, cite **88–89**.

Watch for roman-numeral front matter (introductions often run ix–xxiv) and unnumbered plate pages. If you need an unnumbered page in an otherwise paginated work, MLA's guidance is to [describe it in your prose](https://style.mla.org/citing-an-unnumbered-page/) ("On the copyright page...") rather than invent a number or write "n. pag."

## Case 3: an HTML article from a database

There are no page numbers at all. The database record lists the print version as pp. 70–95.

- **MLA:** include the print page range in the Works Cited entry's first container, but give **no page** in the in-text citation, because your version has none. That's the MLA Style Center's [answer on database page ranges](https://style.mla.org/database-page-ranges/).
- **APA:** for a direct quotation with no page numbers, give other location information. APA's [quotations page](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/quotations) gives a **paragraph number** as its example.
- **Chicago:** if you can get the paginated PDF of the same article, use it and cite printed pages.

## Case 4: a preprint vs the published article

A preprint's pagination and wording often differ from the published version.

- If you cite the **published** version, take the wording and page numbers from the published version. Don't carry over a preprint's page 12.
- If you can only access the preprint, cite the preprint as what you read.
- Check quotations again after switching versions. Copyediting changes words.

## Case 5: e-books

Some e-books have fixed pages that match print. Others reflow, so the "page" depends on your screen and font size.

- **Fixed pages that match print:** cite them like print.
- **Reflowable text:** there's no stable page. Use the locator your style allows (for example, a paragraph number in APA), or switch to a print or PDF edition for the passages you quote.
- **Chicago:** Purdue OWL notes that for self-published e-books, Chicago citations add the application, device, or file format needed to read the book. Check the [Chicago guidance](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/chicago_manual_18th_edition/cmos_formatting_and_style_guide/books.html) for your case.

## Case 6: OCR'd archive photos

You photographed a 1911 letter, three sheets, and ran OCR on it.

- An unpublished letter has no publisher's pagination. Cite it by archive, box, and folder, and add a sheet or folio number if the archive uses one.
- Keep the sheets together as one source in the order they belong, so "sheet 2" means the same thing every time.
- Check OCR text against the image before quoting it. OCR misreads old typefaces and handwriting. See [organizing scanned and archival sources](/guides/organize-scanned-archival-sources-ocr).

## The capture habit that prevents almost all of this

**Write the printed page in your note at the moment you save the quote.** Not later, when you're drafting at 1 a.m. with the PDF closed.

A good note looks like this:

> **p. 205** (printed; viewer p. 6). Supports claim that the 1912 strike was coordinated across ports.

That's eight seconds of work that saves a re-hunt later.

## Worked example: auditing five citations

Pick five page-cited quotations from your draft and check each one:

| Quote | Cited page | Found on printed page | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| "negotiated wage floors..." | 142 | 142 | OK |
| "the first week as orientation..." | 6 | 15 | **Viewer page cited.** Fix to 15 |
| "a privileged site for reflection..." | 50 | 50 | OK |
| "eleven points..." | 9 | 9, but wording differs | **Wording wrong.** Fix the quote |
| "harbor commissions..." | 88 | 88–89 | **Runs across pages.** Fix to 88–89 |

If two or more of your five fail, check every page-cited quote in the draft.
