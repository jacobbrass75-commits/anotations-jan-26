Lawyers talk about the **chain of custody** for evidence: a record showing that the item presented in court is the same item collected, unaltered. A quotation in your paper needs the same thing. The words on page 23 of your draft should be the same words on page 142 of the source, still meaning what they meant there.

The chain has five links. Most quotation errors happen at one of them, and each has a simple safeguard.

## Link 1: Capture

**What breaks:** You retype the quote instead of copying it. You copy from a summary or slide instead of the source. You copy from the preprint and later cite the published version.

**Safeguard:**
- Capture **exact text from the version you'll cite**.
- Capture **enough context**: the sentence before and after, so you can later tell whether it was the author's view or one they were reporting.
- Record the **printed page** right away (see [page numbers for PDFs, scans, and e-books](/guides/citing-page-numbers-pdfs-scans-ebooks)).

## Link 2: Label

**What breaks:** Three months later, you have 300 highlights and no idea why you saved most of them.

**Safeguard:** label each passage by **what job it does in your argument**, and write one line on why. A small, fixed set of labels beats a large, creative one. ScholarMark uses five, and they work well as a scheme in any tool:

| Label | Use it for | Example note |
|---|---|---|
| **Key Quote** | Wording you'll reproduce exactly | "Definition of 'wage floor' for §2. p. 142" |
| **Evidence** | Data, findings, or facts supporting a claim | "1911 strike coordinated across 3 ports. Supports ch. 3 claim. p. 157" |
| **Argument** | The author's interpretation or thesis | "Ortiz argues unions preceded law. I partly disagree (see Price). p. 9" |
| **Methodology** | How they know what they claim | "Payroll records from 2 firms only. Limits generalizing. p. 12" |
| **Your Note** | Your own idea, sparked by the passage | "Compare with Lin on public opinion. Possible §4 opener" |

The **Methodology** label is the one people skip, and the one that most often saves you from over-claiming.

## Link 3: Retrieve

**What breaks:** You remember the quote exists but can't find it, so you paraphrase from memory, or you quote from memory and get two words wrong.

**Safeguard:**
- Keep quotes **searchable by content and by your note**, not just by source.
- Put your **claim words** in the note ("supports ch. 3 coordination claim"). You'll search for your argument, not the author's phrasing.

## Link 4: Place

**What breaks:** You paste the quote, then edit the sentence around it, and the quote gets edited too. Or you trim it with an ellipsis that changes its meaning (see [changing quotations honestly](/guides/changing-quotations-ellipses-brackets)).

**Safeguard:**
- **Paste, don't retype.**
- **Mark the quote** in your draft so you know it's verbatim.
- Introduce it with an accurate signal: "argues," "reports," and "speculates" mean different things.

## Link 5: Verify

**What breaks:** Nothing gets checked, because everything "looked right" when you wrote it.

**Safeguard:** before submitting, re-compare every direct quotation against the source text and check every page number. The [pre-submission quote and citation check](/guides/pre-submission-quote-and-citation-check) has the full procedure.

## Worked example: one quote's journey

*(Invented source: Ortiz, Harbor Cities, 2019.)*

**Capture.** In chapter 5, you find: "Dockworkers' associations negotiated wage floors decades before federal law required them, though only in ports where a single employer dominated." You copy the full sentence, which includes the qualification after the comma, and note "printed p. 142."

**Label.** Key Quote. Note: "Supports §2 claim that unions set wage floors pre-1938. CAUTION: only single-employer ports. p. 142."

**Retrieve.** Six weeks later, drafting §2, you search "wage floor" and find it immediately, with the caution attached.

**Place.** Your first draft reads:

> Unions "negotiated wage floors decades before federal law required them."¹

Your note's CAUTION reminds you that the source limits this to single-employer ports. You revise:

> In ports dominated by a single employer, dockworkers' associations "negotiated wage floors decades before federal law required them."¹

**Verify.** Before submission, you compare the quoted words with the source: an exact match. The page in your footnote is 142: a match. The claim matches the source's scope: yes, after the revision.

Without the CAUTION in the note, the first draft would have gone out. It's a correctly quoted sentence used to support a broader claim than the source makes. That's the most common serious quotation error, and formatting checks never catch it. A [reverse outline evidence audit](/guides/reverse-outline-evidence-audit) is a good way to find these scope mismatches across a whole section.

## Running the chain in ScholarMark

ScholarMark is built around this chain:

- **Capture:** upload the PDF, text, or scan (with OCR) into a project, or clip from the web with the Chrome extension. A highlight stores the source's exact text.
- **Label:** file highlights as Key Quote, Evidence, Argument, Methodology, or Your Note, each with a note.
- **Retrieve:** search across all sources and annotations in the project.
- **Place:** in the writing workspace, quoted text that matches a saved highlight becomes a link back to that passage.
- **Verify:** Verify Paper is an AI review of the compiled draft against your sources. It's designed to cross-check quotes and flag over-claiming, but it can miss things or flag things wrongly. Treat it as a second reader, and keep doing the scope check from the worked example yourself.
