You're reading Author A, who quotes Author B, and B's line is exactly what you need. Can you cite B? All three major styles allow it, and all three would rather you didn't. This guide covers both the mechanics and the judgment call.

## Two different situations

People mix these up, and the styles treat them differently:

1. **Secondary citation.** You want to use B's words or idea, but you've only read them in A.
2. **Quotation within a quotation.** You're quoting A, and A's sentence happens to contain a quotation from B.

## The rule all three styles agree on: get the original if you can

APA's [secondary sources page](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/secondary-sources) says to cite secondary sources "sparingly," for instance when the original is out of print, unavailable, or in a language you don't read, and that "as a matter of good scholarly practice," you should find, read, and cite the primary source.

Purdue OWL's summary of [Chicago 18](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/chicago_manual_18th_edition/cmos_formatting_and_style_guide/books.html) describes "quoted in" as the form Chicago requires when the original is "utterly unavailable."

Why it matters in practice: A may have quoted B out of context, shortened the passage, or translated it. If you cite B through A, you inherit any of A's errors and present them as your own reading.

**Before using a secondary citation, try:**
- your library catalog and databases for the original,
- interlibrary loan, if you have time before the deadline,
- the original's DOI or catalog record (see [how to verify AI-generated citations](/guides/how-to-verify-ai-generated-citations) for lookup tools),
- asking a librarian, who can often find what you can't.

## Situation 1: secondary citation, style by style

**APA 7**, from APA's page: name the original in the text, write "as cited in," and list **only the source you read** in the references.

> (Rabbitt, 1982, as cited in Lyon et al., 2014)

If the original's year is unknown, omit it: "Allport's diary (as cited in Nicholson, 2003)."

**MLA 9:** use "qtd. in." MLA's answer on [indirect sources](https://style.mla.org/citing-indirect-sources/) shows three placements, all citing only the source you read in Works Cited:

> According to Alexander Pope, "A little learning is a dang'rous thing" (qtd. in Damrosch 239).

> As a wise poet once wrote, "A little learning is a dang'rous thing" (Alexander Pope qtd. in Damrosch 239).

The third option puts it in an endnote: "Alexander Pope qtd. in Damrosch 239."

**Chicago 18 (notes):** use "quoted in," citing both works in the note. Purdue OWL's example:

> 9. Ian Hacking, *The Social Construction of What?* (Harvard University Press, 1999), 103, quoted in Manuel DeLanda, *A New Philosophy of Society* (New York: Continuum, 2006), 2.

## Situation 2: quotation within a quotation

You're quoting A, and A's sentence quotes B. MLA's answer on [quotations contained in quotations](https://style.mla.org/quotation-contained-in-a-quotation/) is clear:

- You don't need to reproduce A's own parenthetical citation for B. Drop it.
- You can name B in your prose if that helps the reader.
- The inner quotation marks become single quotation marks.
- You list only A in Works Cited.

MLA's example:

> Goodstein explains, quoting Kuhn's *The Demon of Noontide*, that "[l]iterature does not simply 'mirror man's problems and his attempts to come to terms with them'; it is a privileged site for reflection on the meaning of human experience" (50).

Note the "[l]" in that example. The capital L was lowered to fit the sentence, and the bracket shows the change.

## Worked example: one passage, three styles

*(Invented sources.)* You're reading Grace Lin's 2020 chapter, which quotes a 1934 union pamphlet by "the Gulf Dockworkers' Council" that you can't locate in any library: "No man works the hold alone."

**APA:**
> The council's motto, "No man works the hold alone" (Gulf Dockworkers' Council, 1934, as cited in Lin, 2020, p. 41), framed safety as solidarity.

References: Lin only.

**MLA:**
> The council's motto, "No man works the hold alone" (Gulf Dockworkers' Council qtd. in Lin 41), framed safety as solidarity.

Works Cited: Lin only.

**Chicago:**
> 12. Gulf Dockworkers' Council, pamphlet, 1934, quoted in Grace Lin, "Port Strikes and Public Opinion," in *Labor on the Water*, ed. Samuel Reyes (Riverbend University Press, 2020), 41.

In each case, the reader can see you didn't read the pamphlet yourself. That's the point of the form.

## How to keep this straight in your notes

The real failure isn't formatting. It's forgetting, months later, that you never saw the original. Make it impossible to forget:

- When you save the passage, write "SECONDARY: from Lin p. 41; original not seen" in your note.
- Keep the quoted passage attached to the source you actually read, not to a separate record for the original.
- If you later find the original, read the full passage, check the wording against Lin's, and re-cite directly.

In ScholarMark, highlight the passage in Lin's chapter and put that "SECONDARY" flag in the note. Project search will surface it whenever you search for the quote, so the flag travels with the evidence. When you draft, the quote links back to Lin's chapter, which is where it really came from.
