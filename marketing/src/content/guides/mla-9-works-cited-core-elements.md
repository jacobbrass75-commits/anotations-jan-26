MLA style doesn't ask you to memorize a template for every source type. It gives you one ordered list of **core elements** and the idea of **containers**, and you build every entry from those. Once that clicks, odd sources such as podcasts, archive collections, and app content stop being puzzles.

Sources: the MLA Style Center's [Works Cited quick guide](https://style.mla.org/works-cited/works-cited-a-quick-guide/) and its Ask the MLA answers, linked below, plus Purdue OWL's [MLA 9 overview](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/mla_style/mla_formatting_and_style_guide/mla_formatting_and_style_guide.html). Some Style Center posts were written for the eighth edition and say so at the top. This guide notes where that applies.

## The nine core elements, in order

Each element is followed by the punctuation shown:

1. **Author.**
2. **Title of source.**
3. **Title of container,**
4. **Other contributors,**
5. **Version,**
6. **Number,**
7. **Publisher,**
8. **Publication date,**
9. **Location.**

Skip any element that doesn't apply to your source. MLA's answers on [undated web sources](https://style.mla.org/web-sites-no-publication-date/) and [unnumbered pages](https://style.mla.org/citing-an-unnumbered-page/) both say to omit or describe missing information rather than fill the slot with a placeholder.

## Containers

The MLA quick guide explains that when a source is part of a larger whole, that larger whole is its **container**. A short story is the source, and the anthology is the container. A journal article is the source, and the journal is the container.

Some sources have **two containers**. If you read a journal article through a database, the journal is the first container and the database is the second. Elements 3 through 9 repeat for each container.

## Worked entry 1: a book

*(Invented source for illustration.)*

> Ortiz, Maya. *Harbor Cities: Trade and Labor on the Gulf Coast, 1880–1920*. Riverbend UP, 2019.

- Author: Ortiz, Maya.
- Title of source: the book title, italicized.
- No container, because the book is self-contained.
- Publisher: "UP" abbreviates "University Press," as in MLA's own examples ("Cambridge UP").
- Date: 2019.

In-text citation: (Ortiz 142), with no comma and no "p."

## Worked entry 2: a journal article read through a database

> Price, Daniel. "Wage Floors on the Waterfront." *Journal of Gulf Labor History*, vol. 12, no. 2, 2021, pp. 70–95. *Labor History Online*, `www.example.org/lho/price-wage-floors`.

- **First container:** the journal, with its volume, issue, date, and the page range of the print article.
- **Second container:** the database and its URL. (The database name and URL here are placeholders.)

MLA's [Ask the MLA answer on database page ranges](https://style.mla.org/database-page-ranges/) says that if the database supplies the original print page range, you include it in the first container, even when the version you read has no page numbers. If that HTML version has no page numbers, your in-text citation has no page number either.

## Worked entry 3: a chapter in an edited collection

> Lin, Grace. "Port Strikes and Public Opinion." *Labor on the Water*, edited by Samuel Reyes, Riverbend UP, 2020, pp. 33–58.

- Title of source: the chapter, in quotation marks.
- Container: the book, italicized.
- Other contributors: "edited by Samuel Reyes."
- Location: the chapter's page range.

## Worked entry 4: a web page with no date

> "Dockworkers' Oral Histories." *Gulf Labor Archive*, Gulf Coast Historical Society, `www.example.org/oral-histories`. Accessed 3 Oct. 2026.

- No author is listed, so the entry starts with the title.
- No publication date is listed, so that element is simply omitted. MLA's [answer on undated web sources](https://style.mla.org/web-sites-no-publication-date/) says the same, and suggests adding an access date.
- **Access date:** MLA treats this as optional. Its [access-date answer](https://style.mla.org/access-dates/) (written for the eighth edition) calls it especially useful when a work lacks a publication date or might change. Put it at the end.

For more on undated and authorless sources across all three styles, see [citing web sources with missing author or date](/guides/citing-web-sources-missing-author-date).

## Building an entry for an unusual source

Use the elements as a checklist. Here's an example: a recorded panel talk you watched on a conference's video page.

1. Author: the speaker.
2. Title of source: the talk's title, in quotation marks.
3. Title of container: the conference or the video platform.
4. Other contributors: the moderator, if relevant.
5. Version: none.
6. Number: session number, if one is given.
7. Publisher: the organizing body.
8. Publication date: the date the talk was given or posted.
9. Location: the URL.

Fill in what exists and leave out what doesn't. That procedure is what MLA's system is designed for.

## In-text citations that match

- Use the first element of the entry (usually the author's surname) plus a page: (Ortiz 142).
- With no author, use a shortened title: ("Dockworkers' Oral Histories").
- With no page numbers, cite without one. For a specific unnumbered page in a paginated document, MLA's [answer on unnumbered pages](https://style.mla.org/citing-an-unnumbered-page/) says to describe it in your prose ("On the copyright page...") rather than inventing a number or writing "n. pag."

## Common errors to check

- [ ] The container title is italicized, and the source title is in quotation marks when it's part of a container.
- [ ] Volume and issue are written "vol. 12, no. 2."
- [ ] Page ranges use "pp." in the Works Cited list, but plain numbers in parentheses in the text.
- [ ] Missing elements are omitted, not filled with placeholders.
- [ ] Entries are alphabetized by their first element, with hanging indents.
