Citation generators are useful. They save you from memorizing where the comma goes in an APA reference. But "formatted correctly" and "correct" are different properties, and generators only promise the first.

## What a generator actually does

Take [Grammarly's free citation generator](https://www.grammarly.com/citations) as a representative example. You choose a style (APA, MLA, or Chicago) and a source type: book, journal article, website, newspaper, dissertation, online video, and others, including "Generative AI." You fill in fields such as contributors (author, editor, translator), year, title, publisher, edition, pages, DOI, and URL. It then shows a formatted full citation and an in-text citation.

Reference managers like [Zotero](https://www.zotero.org/support/word_processor_integration) do the same formatting job across thousands of styles, usually from metadata captured automatically from a database or catalog. ScholarMark generates Chicago notes-bibliography, MLA, and APA from metadata you save, and it can try to extract that metadata from the document text.

In every case, the output is **a formatting of the input.**

## What generators don't check

| Question | Does formatting answer it? |
|---|---|
| Is this the right comma, italics, and order for my style? | Yes, if the tool implements the style well |
| Does this work exist? | No, unless you looked it up from a real record |
| Are the author names, year, and pages right? | Only as right as the input |
| Is this the edition I actually read? | No |
| Does the source say what my sentence claims? | No |
| Is my quotation word-for-word? | No |
| Has the article been retracted? | No |

The last four rows are where most real citation problems live. [How to verify AI-generated citations](/guides/how-to-verify-ai-generated-citations) covers checks for them.

## Field by field: where bad input comes from

**Authors.**
- Database records sometimes store names in all capitals or with initials only. Fix capitalization and check that you have every author, in order.
- Watch for editors entered as authors. A chapter's author isn't the book's editor.

**Year.**
- Reprints, paperbacks, and digitized editions carry different dates. Use the date of the version you read.
- Online-first articles may have an earlier online date and a later issue date. Use what your style asks for.

**Title.**
- Copying from a web page can bring along site names ("Title | Publisher") or an inconsistent subtitle.
- Your style's capitalization rules apply. Don't trust the source's own capitalization.

**Source type.**
- Choosing "website" for a journal article you found online gives you a website citation for a journal article. Pick the type of the **work**, not the place you found it. MLA's idea of [containers](/guides/mla-9-works-cited-core-elements) is a useful way to think about this: the journal is the container, and the website or database is a second container, not the source type.

**Pages.**
- An article's full page range (for the reference list) differs from the page you're quoting (for the in-text citation or note).
- Use printed page numbers, not PDF viewer pages. See [page numbers for PDFs, scans, and e-books](/guides/citing-page-numbers-pdfs-scans-ebooks).

**DOI and URL.**
- Make sure the DOI resolves to this exact work. APA, for example, says to [include a DOI for any work that has one](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/references/dois-urls), and to give only the DOI when a work has both a DOI and a URL.

**Empty fields.**
- Most generators quietly drop elements you leave blank. A missing publisher or edition just vanishes from the output, and nothing warns you. Look at the finished citation and ask what should be there that isn't.

## Worked example: same tool, two inputs

*(Invented book.)* You read the 2021 paperback edition of Maya Ortiz's *Harbor Cities: Trade and Labor on the Gulf Coast, 1880–1920*, originally published in 2019.

**Input A**, copied from a bookstore page: author "ORTIZ, MAYA"; title "Harbor Cities | Riverbend University Press"; year 2021; type: website.

**Output A** is a perfectly formatted website citation with the wrong source type, a polluted title, and capitalization the generator may not fix.

**Input B**, from the copyright page of the copy you read: author Maya Ortiz; title *Harbor Cities: Trade and Labor on the Gulf Coast, 1880–1920*; publisher Riverbend University Press; year 2021 (and check whether your style wants the original 2019 date noted); type: book.

**Output B** is right, because the input was right.

The generator did the same job both times. The difference was entirely in what went in.

## When generators are the right tool

- **Short papers with a handful of sources,** where you'll check each entry by eye.
- **One-off citations,** such as an email to an advisor or a slide.
- **Learning a style:** compare the generator's output to the manual's examples.

## When you need more than a generator

- **Long papers,** where the same source is cited dozens of times and must be consistent everywhere.
- **Chicago footnotes,** where you need full first notes and shortened later notes for the same source.
- **Quote-heavy writing,** where the real risk is the quotation and page, not the comma.

In those cases, keep **one metadata record per source** and generate every citation for it from that record. Whether you do it in a reference manager or in ScholarMark, the point is that you fix an error once and it's fixed everywhere.
