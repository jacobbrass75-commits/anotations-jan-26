"Use Chicago" is ambiguous. *The Chicago Manual of Style* has two citation systems, and they look nothing alike on the page. This guide explains both, with examples, and gives you a way to decide.

Sources: the [Chicago-Style Citation Quick Guide](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/tools_citationguide.html) from the University of Chicago Press, and Purdue OWL's [CMOS 18th edition guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/chicago_manual_18th_edition/cmos_formatting_and_style_guide/chicago_manual_of_style_18th_edition.html). The 18th edition was published in 2024.

## The two systems in one sentence each

- **Notes and bibliography (NB):** sources are cited in numbered footnotes or endnotes, keyed to superscript numbers in the text, and usually also listed in a bibliography.
- **Author-date:** sources are cited briefly in the text, usually as (Author Year, page), and each in-text citation matches an entry in a reference list.

Chicago's quick guide notes that the two systems share the same style for authors' names, titles, and other components. The difference is where and how the citation appears.

## What they look like

For illustration, here is an invented book: Maya Ortiz, *Harbor Cities: Trade and Labor on the Gulf Coast, 1880–1920*, Riverbend University Press, 2019.

**Notes and bibliography**

In the text:

> Dockworkers' associations "negotiated wage floors decades before federal law required them."¹

First note:

> 1. Maya Ortiz, *Harbor Cities: Trade and Labor on the Gulf Coast, 1880–1920* (Riverbend University Press, 2019), 142.

Later note for the same source (shortened form):

> 4. Ortiz, *Harbor Cities*, 157.

Bibliography:

> Ortiz, Maya. *Harbor Cities: Trade and Labor on the Gulf Coast, 1880–1920*. Riverbend University Press, 2019.

**Author-date**

In the text:

> Dockworkers' associations "negotiated wage floors decades before federal law required them" (Ortiz 2019, 142).

Reference list:

> Ortiz, Maya. 2019. *Harbor Cities: Trade and Labor on the Gulf Coast, 1880–1920*. Riverbend University Press.

Notice that the date moves up to second position in the author-date reference entry, because that's how readers match it to (Ortiz 2019).

Purdue OWL notes that in the 18th edition, **place of publication is no longer required** unless needed to avoid confusion. Older guides and templates that put "Houston:" before the publisher are following earlier editions.

## Which fields use which

Chicago's own guidance:

- NB "is preferred by many working in the humanities, including literature, history, and the arts." It "can accommodate a wide variety of sources, including unusual ones that don't fit neatly into the author-date system."
- Author-date "is preferred by many in the sciences and social sciences."
- "Most authors choose the system used by others in their field or required by their publisher. Students should check with their instructor."

## How to decide when no one tells you

Work through these in order and stop at the first one that answers it:

1. **Check the assignment, syllabus, or department thesis guide.** Many specify "Chicago notes" or "Chicago author-date." Some just say "footnotes."
2. **Ask your instructor or advisor.** One sentence by email saves a weekend of reformatting.
3. **Look at the journals in your field.** Open three recent articles from journals your advisor reads. Notes or parenthetical dates?
4. **Look at your sources.** Archival documents, manuscripts, interviews, and court records fit NB more naturally, because a note can describe an unusual source and add commentary.
5. **Look at how you argue.** If you often need to say "see also" or explain a source's limits in a citation, NB's notes give you room. If what matters most is when a finding was published, author-date puts the year in front of the reader.

## Worked example: a history paper with archival sources

Your evidence includes a 1911 letter in a county archive. In NB, a note can carry the details a reader needs:

> 7. J. R. Hollis to the Board of Harbor Commissioners, March 3, 1911, box 4, folder 12, Harbor Commission Records, Galveston County Archives.

Author-date can handle archival material too, but citing "(Hollis 1911)" pushes those details elsewhere and reads awkwardly when you cite many letters from the same year. That's one reason historians tend to use NB.

## Worked example: a sociology paper with recent empirical studies

You cite eight survey studies published between 2016 and 2024, and the sequence of findings matters. Author-date lets a reader see the chronology at a glance: "(Lee 2016; Ahmed and Cole 2021; Park 2024)." Footnotes would hide it.

## Switching systems midway

If you switch after drafting:

- **NB to author-date:** each note becomes an in-text citation, and the bibliography becomes a reference list with the date moved up. Any commentary in your notes has to move into the text or be cut.
- **Author-date to NB:** each parenthetical becomes a note, and you need full first-note forms and shortened later forms (see [Chicago shortened notes and ibid. in CMOS 18](/guides/chicago-shortened-notes-ibid-cmos-18)).

A reference manager or citation tool makes this mostly mechanical, as long as your metadata is complete. It can't move your commentary for you.

## A note on tools

Tools differ in which Chicago systems they support. Zotero's style repository includes both. ScholarMark generates **notes-bibliography only**: full notes, shortened subsequent notes, and bibliography entries. It doesn't do author-date. If you need author-date, use a tool that supports it, or format those citations by hand.

Check any generated citation against the manual's examples. Generated output is only as complete as the metadata behind it, and a missing publisher or edition carries straight into your notes.
