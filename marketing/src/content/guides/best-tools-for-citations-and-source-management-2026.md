**Disclosure:** ScholarMark publishes this guide, and ScholarMark appears in it. Every other tool is described from its own documentation, linked inline, and the guide says plainly where ScholarMark isn't the right pick.

Most "best tools" lists rank products against each other as if they all did the same thing. They don't. A reference manager, a literature search engine, an AI notebook, and a drafting workspace solve different problems, and a thesis usually needs more than one of them.

So this guide has no scores and no #1. It's organized by the job you need done. Find your job, read the options, then use the decision checklist at the end.

## Job 1: Store references and cite them inside Word or Google Docs

This is the classic reference manager job. You collect bibliographic records and PDFs, and a word processor plugin inserts citations and builds the bibliography as you write.

**Zotero.** Zotero's site describes it as a free, open-source tool from an independent nonprofit for collecting, organizing, annotating, citing, and sharing research. It runs on Mac, Windows, Linux, iOS, and Android. Its [word processor plugins](https://www.zotero.org/support/word_processor_integration) work in Microsoft Word, LibreOffice, and Google Docs and create what Zotero calls dynamic bibliographies: cite a new item and the bibliography updates. The [homepage](https://www.zotero.org/) claims support for over 9,000 citation styles.

**EndNote.** Clarivate's [EndNote](https://endnote.com/) is a commercial reference manager. Its site lists over 7,000 citation styles, cloud sync, shared libraries, and inserting formatted citations and bibliographies as you write. Many universities hold site licenses, so check with your library before you buy anything.

**Mendeley.** Elsevier describes [Mendeley](https://www.elsevier.com/products/mendeley) as a free reference manager for storing, organizing, noting, sharing, and citing references. It also generates bibliographies and imports from other research software.

**Paperpile.** [Paperpile](https://paperpile.com/) offers a browser extension, a Word plugin, a Google Docs plugin, and BibTeX/LaTeX support. As of this writing, its sign-up page says new accounts can only be created on Chrome, and Safari and Firefox support is in beta.

**Pick this category if:** your main pain is formatting citations correctly across a long document, especially in Word or Google Docs, or you need a style beyond Chicago, MLA, or APA.

## Job 2: Find literature you haven't read yet

Discovery tools search a large scholarly corpus. They are not where you keep your own evidence.

**Elicit.** [Elicit](https://elicit.com/) says it searches over 138 million academic papers and 545,000 clinical trials. It generates research reports and supports screening and data extraction for systematic reviews.

**scite.** [scite](https://scite.ai/) is built around "Smart Citations," which show how later research has supported, challenged, or discussed a paper. It also offers an AI assistant, a browser extension, a Zotero plugin, and an MCP integration for AI assistants.

**Google Scholar.** It's still the default free starting point. Its [help page](https://scholar.google.com/intl/en/scholar/help.html) describes the "Cited by" link for moving forward in time from an older paper.

**Pick this category if:** you don't have your sources yet. Then move what you find into a library tool (Job 1 or Job 5).

## Job 3: Ask questions about documents you already have

**NotebookLM, now Gemini Notebook.** Google's NotebookLM help center now calls the product Gemini Notebook, and notebooklm.google.com redirects to notebook.google.com. According to [Google's source documentation](https://support.google.com/notebooklm/answer/16215270?hl=en), it accepts PDFs, Word files, web URLs, YouTube URLs, audio, images, Google Docs and Slides, and more. Each source can hold up to 500,000 words or 200MB, and free users can include up to 50 sources per notebook. Answers come with in-line citations back to your sources.

**Pick this category if:** you need to understand a stack of reading quickly: summaries, study guides, explanations. It isn't designed as a bibliography formatter or a long-term quote archive. For a detailed comparison, see [ScholarMark vs NotebookLM](/guides/scholarmark-vs-notebooklm).

## Job 4: Make one citation, fast

**Grammarly's citation generator.** Grammarly's [free generator](https://www.grammarly.com/citations) formats APA, MLA, and Chicago entries from fields you fill in or look up.

**ZoteroBib.** Zotero's homepage points to ZoteroBib for a quick bibliography without installing anything.

**Pick this category if:** you need a works-cited list for a short paper. Read [what citation generators check and what they don't](/guides/citation-generators-what-they-check) before you trust the output.

## Job 5: Keep exact quotes connected to their sources while you draft

This is the job ScholarMark was built for, so treat this section as the most biased one.

The gap it addresses: in a long paper, the hard part often isn't formatting the bibliography. It's knowing that the sentence in paragraph 14 of chapter 3 really matches page 212 of the source, in that edition, and still means what you say it means.

What ScholarMark does, specifically:

- You upload PDFs, text files, or images (including phone photos in HEIC format) into a project. Scans can go through OCR.
- You highlight passages and file them as Key Quote, Evidence, Argument, Methodology, or Your Note, each with a note about why it matters.
- You save citation metadata and generate Chicago notes-bibliography, MLA, or APA output.
- When you draft in ScholarMark, quotes that match saved highlights link back to the passage in the source.
- A "Verify Paper" pass uses AI to review a compiled draft against your project sources. It flags quotes that don't match, paraphrases that drift, citations with no matching source, and formatting problems. It's a review aid, not a guarantee. You still check the findings yourself.

What it doesn't do: there's no Word or Google Docs plugin (you export DOCX or copy citations), no BibTeX or RIS import, no literature search engine, and only three citation styles. If any of those is a hard requirement, pair it with a Job 1 tool or choose differently.

## Job 6: Annotate the web or a reading queue

**Hypothesis.** [Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is/) is social annotation: highlights and comments over web pages, aimed largely at courses and LMS integration.

**Readwise Reader.** [Reader](https://readwise.io/read) is a read-it-later app with highlighting for web pages, PDFs, EPUBs, newsletters, RSS, and YouTube transcripts.

**Pick this category if:** your reading is mostly online and you want highlights in one place. For research capture specifically, see [web clipping for academic research](/guides/web-clipping-for-academic-research).

## Worked example: two stacks for two projects

**A history honors thesis.** About 60 sources: archival scans, monographs, a few articles. Chicago notes-bibliography.

- Discovery: library catalog plus Google Scholar.
- Library and quotes: ScholarMark. Archive photos go through OCR, and every quote you might use is filed with a page note.
- Final citation formatting: ScholarMark's Chicago output, checked against the manual. If your department requires a Word plugin workflow, keep Zotero alongside it for the bibliography.

**A psychology literature review.** Over 150 articles, APA 7, co-written with a lab mate in Google Docs.

- Discovery: Elicit or scite.
- References and citing: Zotero or Paperpile, because the Google Docs plugin and shared libraries matter more here than a quote archive.
- Optional: an evidence matrix (see [building a literature review evidence matrix](/guides/literature-review-evidence-matrix)) kept wherever you can search it.

ScholarMark is a strong fit for the first project and optional for the second. Collaboration in Google Docs points toward the reference managers.

## Decision checklist

Answer these before you choose anything:

1. **Do you write in Word or Google Docs with live citations?** If so, you need a Job 1 tool.
2. **Do you need a style other than Chicago, MLA, or APA?** Then use Zotero, EndNote, Mendeley, or Paperpile for formatting.
3. **Are you still finding sources?** Start with Job 2.
4. **Will you quote heavily from scans, archives, or long books?** Exact-quote tracking (Job 5) matters more than usual.
5. **Are you collaborating?** Check sharing features in each tool's own docs. ScholarMark doesn't have shared projects.
6. **Does your course limit AI use?** Read the policy first. Several tools here include AI features you may need to leave off.
