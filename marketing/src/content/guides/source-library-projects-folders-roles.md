Most source organization advice is about *labeling*: tags, colors, statuses. The bigger decision comes first, and it's structural. Where does each source live, and how does the same source get used in more than one place without being duplicated?

## Principle 1: one record per work

Every work you read should exist **once** in your system. If the same book exists as two records, perhaps one uploaded in September and one re-uploaded in January, your notes split between them and your citations can diverge (different years, different editions).

**Practical rule:** before adding a source, search for it. If it's there, reuse it.

## Principle 2: separate the library from the projects

- **The library** is everything you've collected, across all your work.
- **A project** is one output: a paper, a thesis, a chapter, a grant proposal. It *uses* some sources from the library.

The same source can belong to several projects. Your notes about why a source matters can differ by project, while the source itself, with its text and citation metadata, stays the same.

ScholarMark is built this way. Uploaded sources live in your **personal source library** and can be attached to any number of **projects**. Each project records a working thesis or research question and its scope. When you attach a source to a project, you can add **project-specific context**: what the source is for *in this project*.

## Principle 3: choose a folder scheme that matches how you draft

Inside a project, folders should mirror the way you'll **retrieve** sources while writing. Three common schemes:

| Scheme | Folders look like | Best when | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **By section** | Ch1, Ch2, Ch3, Intro | You draft one section at a time | Sources used in several sections need a home; pick the main one |
| **By theme** | Labor law, Strikes, Public opinion | Your argument cuts across sections | Harder to see what a given chapter needs |
| **By source type** | Archive, Books, Articles, Web | Very mixed material, early research | Doesn't help drafting at all |

**Decision rule:** if your outline is stable, organize by section. If it's still moving, organize by theme, and switch to sections once the outline settles. Avoid organizing by source type past the early research stage.

Keep folders shallow. Two levels is plenty. If you're building a third level, use labels or notes instead.

## Principle 4: mark each source's role

Not every source you collect is evidence. Mixing roles leads to two specific mistakes: citing background reading as if it supported a specific claim, and forgetting that a source was only a model for style or structure.

ScholarMark lets you mark a source's **role** in a project:

- **Evidence:** you'll cite it to support specific claims.
- **Background:** it informs your understanding, but you won't lean on it for specific claims.
- **Style reference:** you're studying it for how it's written (structure, tone, argument moves), not for what it says. ScholarMark can analyze a style reference's writing patterns, such as sentence length and vocabulary level, separately from your evidence.

The same work can be evidence in one project and background in another.

## Worked example: a thesis plus a seminar paper

You're writing a senior thesis on port labor (three chapters) and, at the same time, a seminar paper on early labor law that shares some sources.

**Library:** 74 sources, including archive documents, books, articles, and web clips. Each exists once.

**Project A: Thesis.** Working thesis recorded. Folders: Ch1 Context, Ch2 Wage Floors, Ch3 Strikes. Archive letters, Ortiz, and Price are all **Evidence**. A general history of the Gulf Coast is **Background**. An award-winning article in your field, kept for its structure, is a **Style reference**.

**Project B: Seminar paper.** Shares Price and Ortiz with the thesis. Here Ortiz is **Background**, because the paper's argument is about statutes, and Price is **Evidence**. Project context for Price: "Use his statutory timeline, not his strike analysis."

**What this prevents:**
- Ortiz exists once, so a corrected publication year fixes both projects' citations.
- In the seminar paper, Ortiz's role reminds you not to build an argument on him there.
- Notes about strikes stay in the thesis project, so the seminar paper's search results stay clean.

## Lightweight status tracking

Within each project, it helps to know where each source stands. Keep it minimal: *to read*, *read*, *highlighted*, *cited*. If you use more than four or five statuses, you'll stop updating them.

## Common structural mistakes

- **One giant project for everything.** Search results become noisy and roles blur. Split by output.
- **Duplicating a source per project** instead of attaching it. Notes and metadata fragment.
- **Organizing by where you found things** (a database name, "from Prof. X's syllabus"). It's useless when drafting.
- **Never marking background.** Background sources look like evidence six months later.
